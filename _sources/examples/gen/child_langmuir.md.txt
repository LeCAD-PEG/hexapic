# Space-charge-limited diode

<video class="hx-video" controls loop muted playsinline preload="metadata" poster="../../_static/videos/child_langmuir.jpg" src="../../_static/videos/child_langmuir.mp4"></video>

A cathode emits electrons into a vacuum gap, and an anode at the voltage
$+V$ collects them. The charge of the electrons in the gap lowers the
potential in front of the cathode. When the cathode emits more than the gap
can carry, a potential minimum forms there, the slower electrons return to
the cathode, and the current at the anode no longer depends on the emission:
it is limited by the space charge. The case tests the injection of a beam,
the absorption at the walls and the field solve with a biased wall.

## Setup

| | |
|---|---|
| Domain | gap $d = 12.8$ mm along $x$, 0.4 mm along $y$ (periodic) |
| Grid | 256 × 8 cells of $\Delta x = 50$ µm; the problem is 1-D along $x$ |
| Walls | cathode at $x = 0$, grounded; anode at $x = d$, biased at $V = 100$ V (`--voltage`: 50 V and 200 V below) |
| Emission | a `[[beam]]` of electrons from the cathode: the current density $J_s = 120\ \mathrm{A/m^2}$, a half-Maxwellian of the temperature $T = 0.5$ eV. The particles start at $x = \Delta x / 2$. |
| Limit | Child–Langmuir current density $J_{CL} = 14.25\ \mathrm{A/m^2}$ at 100 V: the cathode emits 8.4 times as much |
| Particles | weight 576: about $8\times10^{5}$ macro-particles in the steady state, 1755 injected per step |
| Time step | $3.37\times10^{-12}$ s: the fastest electron crosses 0.4 cells per step |
| Run | 28 800 steps, 15 transit times of an electron across the gap (6.5 ns each); output every 60 steps over the last 10, no charge smoothing |
| Field solver | the direct [strip solver](../../docs/solvers/strip.md), as `type = "auto"` chooses for walls at both $x$ ends |
| Cost | about 80 s on one NVIDIA A100 |

## Running

```bash
python examples/walls/child_langmuir/case.py runs/child_langmuir
build/HEXAPIC runs/child_langmuir/case.toml > runs/child_langmuir/run.log
python examples/walls/child_langmuir/analyze.py runs/child_langmuir
```

`case.py` writes `runs/child_langmuir/case.toml`; with `--run` it also runs
`build/HEXAPIC` on it (`--hexapic` names another binary). `case.toml` beside
the script is the same case. `analyze.py` reads the series and the log of
the run, `run.log`: its `[WallFlux]` line has the number of electrons that
the anode absorbed. It prints the numbers below and writes `results.json`
and the figure into the run's directory. Without a log it takes the current
from the meshes.

| Option of `case.py` | |
|---|---|
| `--voltage V` | the voltage of the anode [V]; the time step and the weight follow it |
| `--run` | run HEXAPIC on the case |
| `--live` | write the frames that HEXAPIC / VIZ shows during the run |

## Results

### Current against theory

Electrons that leave the cathode at rest carry at most the Child–Langmuir
current density

$$
J_{CL} = \frac{4\varepsilon_0}{9}\sqrt{\frac{2e}{m_e}}\,\frac{V^{3/2}}{d^2} .
$$

The electrons of this case leave the cathode with a half-Maxwellian of the
temperature $T$, as those of a hot cathode do, and the limit is higher.
Langmuir's theory gives it. The potential has a minimum $\phi_m < 0$ at
$x_m$. Electrons with less energy than $-e\phi_m$ return to the cathode, and
the current density $J = J_s \exp(e\phi_m/T)$ passes. With
$\eta = e(\phi - \phi_m)/T$ and $\xi = (x - x_m)/\ell$, Poisson's equation is

$$
\frac{d^2\eta}{d\xi^2} = \frac{1}{2}\,e^{\eta}\left[1 \mp \mathrm{erf}\sqrt{\eta}\,\right],
\qquad \ell^2 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 T}{2 e J}\sqrt{\frac{2T}{\pi m_e}} ,
$$

with the upper sign beyond the minimum and the lower sign before it, and
$\eta = d\eta/d\xi = 0$ at the minimum. The solution must reach
$\eta = \ln(J_s/J)$ at the cathode and $\eta = \ln(J_s/J) + eV/T$ at the
anode, a distance $d$ apart. This condition gives $J$. `analyze.py`
integrates the equation and solves for $J$; for $T \to 0$ the result is
$J_{CL}$.

The measured current density is the charge of the electrons that the anode
absorbed over the last 10 transit times, per time and area. The runs at
50 V and 200 V are the same case with `--voltage`:

```bash
for v in 50 100 200; do
    python examples/walls/child_langmuir/case.py runs/child_langmuir_$v --voltage $v
    build/HEXAPIC runs/child_langmuir_$v/case.toml > runs/child_langmuir_$v/run.log
done
python examples/walls/child_langmuir/analyze.py runs/child_langmuir_*
```

![The current density against the anode voltage: HEXAPIC's three points on the line of Langmuir's theory, above the Child-Langmuir law; and the potential across the gap at 100 V](../../_examples/walls/child_langmuir/figures/current.png)

*Left: the current density at the anode against its voltage. Right: the
potential across the gap at 100 V; HEXAPIC is the dashed line on that of
Langmuir's theory.*

| $V$ [V] | 50 | 100 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|
| HEXAPIC, $J$ [A/m²] | $7.2553 \pm 0.0001$ | $18.028 \pm 0.001$ | $46.844 \pm 0.002$ |
| Langmuir's theory, $J$ [A/m²] | 7.208 | 17.959 | 46.767 |
| Langmuir's theory, emission at $\Delta x/2$ [A/m²] | 7.287 | 18.091 | 47.016 |
| Child–Langmuir, $J_{CL}$ [A/m²] | 5.037 | 14.245 | 40.292 |
| HEXAPIC over Langmuir's theory | 1.0066 | 1.0038 | 1.0017 |

The uncertainty is the standard error of the mean over ten blocks of output
steps. The current is steady: its scatter between the output steps is 0.1 %.

HEXAPIC's current is 0.2 % to 0.7 % above Langmuir's theory. The difference
comes from the beam, which starts its particles half a cell in front of the
cathode. With the emission plane at $\Delta x/2$ the theory gives the third
row, and HEXAPIC is 0.4 % below it. A run with 512 cells along $x$ at an
emission of $3\,J_{CL}$ gave 0.21 % above the theory, where 256 cells gave
0.33 %.

Over the three voltages the current grows as $V^{1.345}$, and Langmuir's
theory gives $V^{1.349}$. The exponent 3/2 of the Child–Langmuir law is the
limit of a cold emission, $T \ll eV$. At $T = 0.5$ eV the current is 16 % to
44 % above $J_{CL}$.

The emission must have a thermal spread for this comparison. A beam of cold
electrons with a drift of 1 eV and a current density of $3\,J_{CL}$, at
100 V, did not reach a steady state: the potential minimum oscillated, the
current at the anode varied by 13 % (standard deviation) between the output
steps, and its mean was $1.15\,J_{CL}$.

### Potential against theory

For electrons that start at rest the potential is
$\phi(x) = V (x/d)^{4/3}$. Langmuir's solution above gives the potential
with the minimum in front of the cathode. The measured potential is the mean
over $y$ and over the output steps.

| At 100 V | HEXAPIC | Langmuir's theory | emission at $\Delta x/2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potential minimum $\phi_m$ [V] | $-1.16$ | $-0.95$ | $-1.20$ |
| Its position $x_m$ [mm] | 0.35 | 0.34 | 0.36 |
| Largest $\lvert\phi - \phi_\mathrm{theory}\rvert / V$ | | 0.0025 | 0.0010 |

The potential follows Langmuir's solution within 0.25 % of $V$. The largest
difference is at the minimum, which is 0.2 V deeper than in the theory
because the emission plane is half a cell inside the gap. The profile
$V (x/d)^{4/3}$ differs from the measured one by up to 5 % of $V$.

## References

The space-charge-limited current of a planar diode whose electrons leave the
cathode at rest: {cite:t}`child1911` and {cite:t}`langmuir1913`.

The diode whose cathode emits a half-Maxwellian, with the potential minimum
in front of it: {cite:t}`langmuir1923`. The case takes no numbers from that
paper. `analyze.py` integrates Poisson's equation for this electron
distribution itself (its docstring gives the equations) and solves for the
current; for a cold emission its result is the Child–Langmuir current.

The case has no data from other codes.
